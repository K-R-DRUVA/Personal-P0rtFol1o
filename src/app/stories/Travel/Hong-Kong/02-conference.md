# The Morning of the Conference

The morning of the conference had finally arrived.

For once in my life...

I actually woke up on time.

I brushed my teeth, got my clothes ready, and jumped into the shower, thinking everything was going according to plan.

Well...

life had other plans.

I turned on the shower and quickly checked the water temperature with my hand.

It felt fine.

So naturally, I turned around and let the water hit my back.

Big mistake.

Somehow the temperature suddenly shot up.

Boiling hot water landed straight on my back.

I jumped out of the shower like I'd just stepped on a landmine.

For the next couple of minutes, I was lying on the bed, squirming around and wondering how I had managed to injure myself before the conference had even started.

Eventually, I gave up.

I took the quickest cold-water bath of my life, got dressed, grabbed my conference bag, and rushed out of the hotel.

By the time I reached the conference venue...

breakfast was already over.

Even worse...

the official group photo had already been taken.

My heart sank.

This was my first international conference, and somehow I had already managed to miss the one picture everyone would probably keep.

I found Jessica, one of the organizers, and nervously explained what had happened.

Instead of getting annoyed, everyone was incredibly understanding.

They reassured me that it was completely fine and helped me figure out where I needed to be.

That was the moment my nervousness started disappearing.

Until then, everyone had just been strangers.

But from that point onwards...

they became people who genuinely wanted me to have a good experience.

---

# Presentation

By the time my presentation started, I had somehow managed to survive the hot shower, missing breakfast, and rushing into the conference hall.

Now came the actual reason I had travelled all the way to Hong Kong.

![Presenting my research paper at the conference.](/images/stories/presentation.jpeg)

*Presenting my research paper at the conference.*


Standing on that stage felt different.

This wasn't another classroom presentation.

This wasn't another college seminar.

People in that room had travelled from different countries to attend the conference.

For the first few minutes, everything went according to plan.

Then...

my mind went completely blank.

Not for long.

Just enough for me to realize...

> "Well... this isn't ideal."

I paused.

Took a deep breath.

Looked at my slides again.

Collected my thoughts.

And continued.

Nobody laughed.

Nobody interrupted.

The world didn't fall apart.

Ironically...

Dad had been right.

> "Nothing is going to happen if you mess up."

I finished the presentation, answered the questions that followed, and walked off the stage feeling relieved.

Looking back now, I don't even remember the questions people asked.

I remember something much more important.

I learned that it's perfectly okay to pause.

You don't have to fill every second with words.

Sometimes...

taking one deep breath is all it takes to get back on track.

---

# The Cab Ride

After the conference ended, it was finally time to head back to the hotel.

By then, I had already made a small group of friends Boom, Japan (yes, that was his nickname), and Prof.Air and Prof.Cat from Thailand.

Naturally...

everyone wanted to travel back with the people they'd spent the entire day with.

The cabs could only take four passengers at a time, and one seat in every cab had to be reserved for a conference volunteer who knew the way back to the hotel.

I ended up in a cab with Yuchen, the conference chair, and a Nigerian teacher.

The Thai group managed to fit perfectly into another cab.

Everything was sorted.

Or so we thought.

Just before the doors closed, one of the conference volunteers realised that someone from the Thai cab had to switch so a guide could ride with them.

The door opened again.

Boom looked around.

Everyone looked at Boom.

Boom realised...

he was the chosen sacrifice.

He quietly climbed out of the cab.

His three Thai friends stayed where they were.

A few seconds later...

the doors closed.

And just like that...

Boom disappeared into another cab.

Alone.

Surrounded by a complete set of strangers.

The rest of us couldn't stop laughing.

Boom just smiled, waved at us, and said,

> "See you back."

Sometimes...

you're not unlucky enough to miss the cab.

You're unlucky enough to be reassigned.

And just like that...

our perfectly planned cab ride disappeared.

While all this was happening, I was helping Yuchen load his suitcase into the boot.

By the time I finished and got into the cab...

I realized I had somehow ended up sitting between Yuchen and a teacher from Nigeria.

I didn't mind.

But judging by the expression on my face...

everyone else could tell I was a little nervous.

The teacher looked at yuchen who was on the other side of me and said,

> "He is the man of the hour,We have to keep him warm."

I remember thinking,

> "Well... that wasn't exactly the icebreaker I was expecting."

Everyone burst into laughter.

And somehow...

the awkwardness disappeared before the cab had even started moving.

---

# The Chinese Banquet

A few minutes later, we finally reached the hotel.

I genuinely thought we'd have a quick dinner, chat for a bit, and head back to our rooms after an exhausting day.

I couldn't have been more wrong.

The conference organizers had arranged a traditional Chinese banquet for all the delegates.

The moment I walked into the dining hall, I just stood there for a second taking everything in.

![The traditional Chinese banquet that welcomed all the conference delegates.](/images/stories/chinese-banquet.jpg)

*Our first glimpse of a traditional Chinese banquet.*

*Looking back at this photo, Boom's expression makes it look like he already knew dinner was about to become an adventure for me.*

Instead of small individual tables, there were huge round tables spread across the hall, each seating ten to twelve people.

In the middle of every table was a large rotating glass tray.

I had only seen something like that in movies.

Every few minutes, waiters would walk in carrying massive dishes and place them right in the centre of the table.

A whole fish.

![Whole fish served at the banquet.](/images/stories/fish.jpg)

Then came the roasted duck.

![Roasted duck.](/images/stories/duck.jpg)

Followed by crispy pork that would eventually become my favourite dish of the evening.

![Roasted pork.](/images/stories/pork.jpg)

Then came vegetables...

![Vegetables.](/images/stories/vegetables.jpg)

...and finally, soup.

![Soup.](/images/stories/soup.jpg)


Everything was shared.

Whenever someone wanted something, they'd gently rotate the glass tray until the dish reached them, take what they wanted, and rotate it for the next person.

It was one of those tiny cultural differences that immediately stood out to me.

Back home, everyone usually has their own plate of food.

Here...

the entire meal felt like a shared experience.

Everyone around the table was talking.

People were introducing themselves.

Passing dishes to one another.

Making sure nobody missed out on anything.

It didn't feel like a formal conference dinner anymore.

It felt like a bunch of strangers slowly becoming friends over food.

---

# The Pork That Started It All

Now before I tell you about the absolute disaster that followed...

I have to appreciate one thing.

The roasted pork.
![Slice of Pork](/images/stories/slice_of_pork.jpg)

To this day, I still think it was one of the best things I've ever eaten.

The skin was unbelievably crispy.

You could actually hear the crunch.

Then, as you bit further in, the meat became softer...

juicier...

and almost melted in your mouth.

I don't think I'd ever had pork cooked like that before.

Back in India, whenever my Muslim friends ask me about Hong Kong, I somehow always end up describing this roasted pork in painful detail.

I'm pretty sure I've made more than one of them regret asking.

Ironically...

out of everything served that evening...

that was probably the only dish my taste buds completely agreed with.

Everything else...

they were still trying to negotiate.

---

# Two Chopsticks... Why?

Just when I thought I had figured out how this dinner worked...

I looked down at my place setting.

There were bowls.

Tiny plates.

Tea cups.

A spoon.

And...

two pairs of chopsticks.

One pair was red.

The other was black.

I remember staring at them for a good few seconds.

> "Why do I need two?"

Then I looked around the table.

To my surprise...

I wasn't the only confused one.

Even some of the delegates from mainland China were looking at the chopsticks with the exact same expression.

Apparently, they had never seen two differently coloured chopsticks at a banquet before either.

For a brief moment...

I felt strangely relieved.

> "Finally... something even they don't know."

Naturally, someone called one of the waitresses over.

She smiled, pointed at the chopsticks, and explained that one pair was meant for serving food from the common dishes onto your own bowl, while the other pair was meant for actually eating.

Simple.

At least...

that's what everyone else thought.

---

# My First Time Holding Chopsticks

Now here's the catch.

I was probably the only Indian sitting at that table who had never held a pair of chopsticks before.

I'd seen them.

Anime.

Ninja Hattori.

Movies.

Restaurants.

But actually holding them...

that was a completely different experience.

Everyone else around me had grown up using chopsticks.

The delegates from Thailand had been eating with them their whole lives.

The Chinese delegates obviously knew exactly what they were doing.

And then there was me...

holding two wooden sticks like I was trying to solve a mechanical engineering problem.

At that moment, I suddenly remembered one of my friends back in India.

She had been obsessed with Japanese food for years.

She'd invited me countless times to come try authentic Japanese food with her.

Every single time...

I'd made up some excuse.

Sitting there in Hong Kong, struggling to even hold chopsticks properly, I couldn't help but think,

> "I really should've gone with her."

Still...

I convinced myself I'd figure it out.

How hard could two sticks possibly be?

I was about to find out.

---

# Confidence Is a Dangerous Thing

Now that I knew which pair of chopsticks was meant for serving and which pair was meant for eating, I confidently told myself,

> "Easy."

> "Just don't mix them up."

I looked at the red pair.

Then the black pair.

I repeated the colour code in my head a couple of times.

> "Red for serving... black for eating."

Or was it...

> "Black for serving... red for eating?"

You know where this is going.

Within the first five minutes...

I'd already forgotten.

I somehow managed to convince myself that black was for serving and red was for eating.

No one noticed.

Or at least...

I hoped no one noticed.

---

# Four Chopstick Coaches

By this point, I had already become good friends with Boom, Japan (yes, that was still his nickname), and the two teachers from Thailand.

Unfortunately for them...

they had unknowingly signed up to become my personal chopstick coaches for the evening.

The moment they saw me holding the chopsticks...

they immediately knew I had absolutely no idea what I was doing.

The coaching session began.

> "Hold it like a pen."

I tried.

Didn't work.

> "Keep the gap between the chopsticks smaller."

I tried that too.

Still didn't work.

> "Don't grip them so tightly."

Somehow...

that made it even worse.

Every piece of food had developed a personal grudge against me.

---

# Meat Wasn't the Problem

To be fair...

meat wasn't actually that difficult.

It was big enough to grab.

Sometimes it slipped.

Sometimes I dropped it.

But eventually...

I'd get lucky.

The real villains of the evening...

were the vegetables.

They had been stir-fried in soy sauce.

They were shiny.

They were slippery.

And they absolutely refused to cooperate.

Every time I thought I had finally picked one up...

it would perform what can only be described as an Olympic-level escape and land right back onto the plate.

At this point, my table had stopped eating and had started watching.

Not because they were making fun of me.

They were genuinely trying to help.

Everyone had their own technique.

Everyone had their own advice.

None of it worked.

---

# The Worst Advice Ever Given

Finally...

After watching me struggle for what must have felt like an eternity...

one of the teachers finally gave up and said,

> "Why don't you just use both chopsticks together and stab it?"    

it sounded like a brilliant idea.

No balancing.

No slipping.

No frustration.

Just...

poke.

I looked at him.

He looked at me.

I did it.

The silence that followed...

told me everything.

Within seconds someone politely stopped me and explained that stabbing food with chopsticks was considered bad manners.

Apparently...

I'd discovered yet another unwritten rule.

At this point I had lost count of how many cultural mistakes I'd made during a single dinner.

And the evening...

wasn't even over yet.

---

# The Final Boss

Just when I thought the worst was over...

the waiters arrived with the final dish.

Fried rice.
![Fried Rice](/images/stories/fried_rice.jpg)

Now you guys might be wondering,

> "Out of everything... fried rice?"

Exactly.

I thought the same thing.

The meat had already tested my patience.

The vegetables had completely destroyed my confidence.

Surely fried rice would be easy.

I couldn't have been more wrong.

The waiter placed a huge bowl of fried rice in the centre of the table.

Everyone served themselves.

Trying to fit in, I served myself exactly the way I'd seen in anime.

A nice little bowl...

with a perfect mound of rice sitting on top.

For a second, I even admired it.

> "That actually looks pretty good."

---

# The Waiter Almost Had a Heart Attack

Everything was perfectly normal.

Until...

I did something that made the waiter almost scream.

Without thinking...

I pushed my chopsticks straight into the bowl of rice so they stood upright.

The entire table froze.

The waiter immediately rushed towards me and said something in Cantonese that I obviously didn't understand.

For a second I genuinely thought,

> "What have I done now?"

The Chinese delegate sitting next to me quickly leaned over and explained.

Apparently, sticking chopsticks upright into a bowl of rice resembles incense offered to the dead during funerals.

It's considered extremely unlucky.

So there I was...

on my very first authentic Chinese dinner...

accidentally inviting bad luck to the entire restaurant.

I apologised immediately.

Again.

At this point apologising had become part of the meal.

---

# Wait... Which Chopsticks Was I Using?

As if that wasn't enough...

someone across the table suddenly looked at me and asked,

> "Weren't you using the serving chopsticks to eat?"

My heart stopped.

I looked down.

Then I looked at both pairs of chopsticks.

Then back at everyone else.

Somewhere along the way...

I had completely mixed them up.

The colour code I'd spent five minutes memorising had officially disappeared from my brain.

I don't even remember who pointed it out.

I just remember wanting the table to swallow me whole.

---

# Too Much Anime

Now if you've watched anime...

you'll know exactly what happens whenever a character makes a mistake.

They immediately stand up.

Bow.

And apologise.

So...

that's exactly what I did.

I stood up.

Bent forward.

And apologised as dramatically as I possibly could.

> "Sumimasen!"

![Sumimasen](/images/stories/tenor.gif)


The entire table burst into laughter.

Someone quickly pulled me back into my chair and said,

> "No, no... don't do that here."

Apparently making such a formal bow at the dinner table wasn't appropriate either.

Perfect.

Another cultural mistake added to the collection.

One of the teachers then smiled and said,

> "Don't worry. I have a cousin who gets confused exactly like this."

For the first time that evening...

I realised I wasn't the only chaotic person in the world.

Everyone laughed.

I laughed.

The waiter eventually brought me another set of chopsticks.

It felt like my dinner had officially been reset.

And somehow...

after surviving every possible cultural mistake I could make in a single evening...

I finally managed to finish my meal.

Jessica happened to point her camera at me right then.

![Holding my chopsticks like I'd mastered them.](/images/stories/chopsticks-group.jpg)

*The confidence of a man who believed he'd finally conquered chopsticks.*

Looking back...

that photograph still makes me laugh.

Because for those few seconds...

I genuinely felt like I'd earned them.

---
# A Final Toast

Just before everyone began leaving the dining hall...

the conference chair made his way around the room, stopping at every table to raise a toast.

Dinner had almost come to an end.

The plates were nearly empty.

The conversations slowly faded away as he reached our table.

![The conference chair making his way around the banquet hall, sharing a final toast with every table.](/images/stories/conference-chair-toast.jpg)

*The conference chair making his way around the banquet hall, sharing a final toast with every table.*

He thanked all of us for travelling from different parts of the world...

for sharing our research...

and for making the conference such a memorable experience.

It wasn't a long speech.

Just a few heartfelt words.

About research.

About friendship.

And about the people we'd met over the last couple of days.

By then...

everyone had stopped eating.

Everyone was listening.

![Our table listening as the conference chair shared a few final words.](/images/stories/table-toast.jpg)

*Our table listening to the conference chair before raising our cups.*

Then...

he raised his cup.

Without anyone saying a word...

the rest of us did the same.

![One final toast before everyone went their separate ways.](/images/stories/banquet-final-toast.JPG)

*One final toast before everyone went their separate ways.*

The speech ended.

But nobody seemed to be in a hurry to leave.

People stayed back.

Talking.

Laughing.

Sharing stories.

Taking photographs.

Exchanging contacts.

Promising to meet again someday.

Looking around the table...

I realised how different all of us were.

Students.

Professors.

Researchers.

Teachers.

People from countries I'd only ever seen on a map.

Yet somehow...

sharing one meal had made everyone feel strangely familiar.

Looking back...

I don't think the banquet was memorable because of the food.

Or because I embarrassed myself with chopsticks.

It was memorable because of the people sitting around that table.

It's funny how sharing a meal with strangers can make them feel like friends.

The next morning...

we'd all be heading in different directions.

Back to different universities.

Different countries.

Different lives.

But for one evening...

we all sat around the same table.























# A Cold Walk Back

Dinner was finally over.

For the first time all evening...

we stepped outside.

The moment the hotel doors opened...

a freezing gust of wind hit us from every direction.

I'd never expected Hong Kong to be that cold.

Within seconds, I was shivering.

People were taking turns pulling Yuchen's suitcase.

we walked  a couple hundred meters untill the bus stand.

At some point...

I did the only thing my freezing brain could think of.

I grabbed her suitcase, ducked my head behind it, and used it as a tiny shield against the wind.

Did it actually help?

Probably not.

Did I look ridiculous?

Absolutely.

But in that moment...

I wasn't thinking about dignity.

I just wanted to survive the walk back.
![Trying to hide behind Yuchen's suitcase](/images/stories/yuchen-suitcase.jpeg)
*My very sophisticated strategy for surviving the Hong Kong wind.*
